> **In short.** The story of AI improving itself eventually runs through the physical world: chips, power plants, grids, robots, and robots that build more robots. The serious growth models say raw materials won't stop this, and on geology they are probably right. But they compare demand against what is *in the ground*. The real constraint is how fast mines, refineries and magnet plants can *grow their output* while demand doubles every year or two. Run that race and two things fall out. First, adding supply buys surprisingly little time: if robot production doubles every eighteen months, doubling magnet supply delays the crunch by under two years. Second, every major material shock of 2026 that I examined came from a chokepoint — export controls, a war, or a by-product of some other industry — not from scarcity. Those chokepoints are where the next few years are decided, and the levers are held by more countries than China.

## The loop

The usual telling goes like this. Better models earn more money. The money buys more compute. More compute trains better models. Somewhere along the way the models get good enough to run factories, and the factories start building robots, power plants, chip fabs and more factories. A fab becomes capital that learns: each doubling of output makes the next unit cheaper.

Every loop runs at the speed of its slowest stage. Training a model takes months. Building a data centre takes a year or two. A copper mine takes, on average, about seventeen years from discovery to first production<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>. So the question I want to answer is a narrow one: *when the loop gets physical, which inputs learn slowest, and who controls them?*

## What the growth models assume

The most careful work on this question comes from people who take fast takeoff seriously, and it is worth stating their case fairly, because on the big picture I think they are right.

Forethought's *Industrial Explosion* estimates that a robot economy could initially double in about a year, and sets resources aside for simplicity. Their footnote is careful: "It's possible that resource constraints are a bottleneck, and this is an important area for further research, but our guess is that they won't be… Historically, resource bottlenecks have never capped GDP growth." And then the caveat that matters for this post: "That doesn't preclude resources being a bottleneck along the way."<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> *AI 2027* has its robot economy double in under a year "so long as it didn't start to run out of raw materials", and answers in a footnote that by then superintelligences "will have prospected new mines and developed new technologies"<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>. Damon Binder's input–output model of the US economy is the most quantitative: he scales mining costs for falling ore grades metal by metal and finds only modest effects on growth, because "mining is only about 1% of output, so even large cost increases do little to slow overall growth"<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>. Epoch's study of robot production finds that the only component that binds today is precision gear reducers, which cap output at roughly half a million humanoids a year<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>.

I agree with the core of this. The crust is not running out. Known copper endowment grew about 25-fold over the twentieth century as exploration and technology improved<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup>, and careful accounting of energy-transition demand finds it below known resources for almost every mineral; the problem is building mines and refineries fast enough<sup><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup>.

But three things are missing from these models, by construction:

1. **Stocks, not flows.** They test cumulative demand against reserves. The question that matters during a takeoff is how fast annual output can grow. Mines grow a few percent a year; a robot fleet doubling every year grows 100%.
2. **A closed, friendly economy.** Binder's model is a closed economy: zero exports, and the roughly 1% of inputs no US industry makes are folded into domestic ones<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>. Epoch's chip analysis stops at the chip<sup><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup>. *AI 2027* treats geopolitics only through chips. None of them can represent an export ban, a refinery that exists in only one country, or a missile strike on a gas plant.
3. **The transition window.** Binder's own appendix flags rare earths as the awkward case: dysprosium is "the most binding element", and his answer is to mine far more ore than you need and discard the rest<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>. That works once the industrial base exists. Getting there is exactly the part I care about.

The published minerals studies have the opposite blind spot. S&P Global, the IEA, Wood Mackenzie and the Payne Institute take refining concentration and by-products seriously, but every one of them translates gigawatts of data centres into tonnes of metal along a smooth path that plateaus — S&P assumes about 30 GW of new capacity a year; Wood Mackenzie expects additions to peak at 30–33 GW a year in the early 2030s<sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup>. None models a loop that feeds on itself. This post sits in the gap between the two literatures.

## Exponentials against mines

This is the core of the argument, and the easiest way to see it is to play with it. Pick a demand that doubles on a fixed schedule — humanoid robots, or data-centre capacity — and a supply that grows a few percent a year. Ask when demand outgrows the share of supply it can realistically claim.

<figure>
  <MaterialRace />
  <figcaption>Exponential demand against slowly growing supply. Log scale: a straight line is constant growth. Defaults are drawn from the sources in the notes under each tab; the doubling time is the hypothesis being tested, not a forecast.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two things fall out of the arithmetic, whatever numbers you choose.

**More supply buys only a little time.** If demand doubles every $T_d$ years and supply grows at rate $g$, then doubling the supply available pushes the crunch back by

$$
\Delta t = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln 2 / T_d - \ln(1+g)}
$$

years. For robots doubling every 18 months against magnet supply growing 8% a year, that is about 1.8 years. Ten times more supply buys about six. Under exponential demand, each extra doubling of supply buys only the same short delay.

**The date is set mostly by the doubling time.** Take humanoid production of 75,000–90,000 in 2026<sup><a href="#ref-18">18</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-19">19</a></sup> (I round to 100,000), 2.5 kg of neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnet per robot, and today's roughly 226 kt of NdFeB magnets a year<sup><a href="#ref-20">20</a></sup>. Holding supply fixed, a one-year doubling takes robots to a fifth of today's magnet output around 2033 and all of it around 2036. A two-year doubling pushes that to about 2041 and 2046. At the chart's defaults — eighteen-month doubling, robots claiming 30% of a supply that grows 8% a year — the crunch lands around 2040.

Is a one-year doubling fanciful? Not for the next decade. Goldman Sachs expects 75,000 humanoids in 2026 and 6.5 million in 2035; Bank of America expects 90,000 rising to 10 million<sup><a href="#ref-18">18</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-19">19</a></sup>. Both imply production doubling every 1.3–1.4 years until 2035, where their forecasts end. CRU's base case reaches about 151 million robots a year in 2040, needing about 363 kt of magnets — around 160% of today's entire market<sup><a href="#ref-20">20</a></sup>. *AI 2027*'s milestone of a million new robots a month (of all kinds) needs 24–36 kt of magnets a year, about 11–16% of today's market: it fits. The wall is roughly three doublings later. The mainstream and the takeoff stories agree until about 2035; they disagree about what happens after.

### Where magnets actually bind

Not at the mine. Outside China, the refined rare-earth oxide that goes into magnets (neodymium-praseodymium, or NdPr) is currently in surplus: it fell to about $95/kg in August, below the $110/kg floor the US Defense Department guarantees MP Materials and Lynas, because there are not enough magnet factories to buy it<sup><a href="#ref-21">21</a></sup>. The IEA calls magnet production "the main bottleneck for supply diversification"; China held about 91% of refining and 94% of sintered magnet output in 2024<sup><a href="#ref-22">22</a></sup>. The constraint is midstream — the steps between ore and finished part: turning oxide into metal, alloying it and pressing magnets, which take a few years and specialised equipment to build.

The heavy rare earths that let magnets survive heat — dysprosium and terbium — are tighter. Lynas, the only commercial separator of them outside China, produces a few tens of tonnes a year (8 t in its March quarter)<sup><a href="#ref-23">23</a></sup>, against capacity of about 290 t if it could get the feedstock<sup><a href="#ref-24">24</a></sup>. At around 57 g of dysprosium-equivalent per humanoid<sup><a href="#ref-25">25</a></sup>, 60 t is enough for roughly a million robots a year built outside China — if nothing else used it. Robots need much less of it per kilogram of magnet than electric cars do, which helps; globally, a single year of electric-car production already uses as much heavy rare earth as 26 million humanoids would<sup><a href="#ref-25">25</a></sup>.

### What binds before magnets

In 2026, robots run out of gears and hands before they run out of magnets. Epoch finds precision reducers cap humanoid output at around half a million a year<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>; a single Japanese firm, Harmonic Drive Systems, holds about 80% of the strain-wave gear market<sup><a href="#ref-26">26</a></sup>. Tesla's Optimus ramp has been slowed by fragile hands and by gear and motor suppliers struggling with quality at volume<sup><a href="#ref-27">27</a></sup>. These are manufacturing problems, and they move fast. But design choices trade one constraint for another: direct-drive joints use more magnet and fewer gears, and as robots gain joints, magnet per robot rises rather than falls<sup><a href="#ref-28">28</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-25">25</a></sup>.

### Copper

Copper is where scope matters most. Inside a data centre, the copper runs to about 12–47 tonnes per megawatt<sup><a href="#ref-29">29</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup>. Once you count the facility's share of new grid, the total is roughly 2.8 times higher: about 64% of the copper tied to AI data centres sits in transmission and distribution<sup><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup>. S&P puts data centres plus their power infrastructure at about 1.1 Mt of copper in 2025, rising to 2.5 Mt by 2040, against roughly 28 Mt of refined demand<sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>.

At forecasters' growth rates, that is a price story, not a wall, and there are honest reasons for scepticism. Only 35–50% of announced data-centre capacity is reaching power on schedule<sup><a href="#ref-30">30</a></sup>. Nvidia's 800-volt DC power design cuts conductor copper substantially, and fibre is replacing copper interconnects<sup><a href="#ref-31">31</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-32">32</a></sup>. Copper only binds in the copper tab above if data-centre additions keep doubling — which no forecaster expects, and which is exactly what the recursive-improvement story predicts.

## Cost share and weak links

Why does a GPU rack not care what copper costs? Because materials are a rounding error in its price. Copper is somewhere between 0.5% and 6% of data-centre capital spending, depending on whose estimate you use<sup><a href="#ref-33">33</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-34">34</a></sup>; helium used in chipmaking is worth about $1 billion a year<sup><a href="#ref-35">35</a></sup>. This is Binder's argument: if one input that is 1% of costs gets ten times more expensive, total costs rise about 10%.

The economics literature has a sharper answer than "but materials matter". When an input is essential and has no substitute, its effect is not proportional to its cost share. Baqaee and Farhi show that these non-linearities roughly tripled the macroeconomic impact of the 1970s oil shocks<sup><a href="#ref-36">36</a></sup>. Aghion, Jones and Jones make the related point that growth is limited "not by what we do well but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve"<sup><a href="#ref-37">37</a></sup>. And Akimitsu notes the practical version: when the cost share is near zero, the threat is not price but *availability*<sup><a href="#ref-38">38</a></sup>. Gallium is a negligible share of GDP and close to 100% of a gallium-nitride power chip. A ban makes it unavailable at any price.

So the thesis of this post is not that materials are a wall. It is that they are a **speed limit**: the slow, essential inputs set the pace of the transition and take a rising share of its cost, and where they are concentrated, they decide who goes first.

## 2026: a year of chokepoints

If materials were going to bind on geology, 2026 would have shown it. Instead, every price shock had a different cause.

- **Helium.** Iranian strikes on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex in March took out about a third of world helium supply<sup><a href="#ref-45">45</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>; QatarEnergy says the damage sidelines 17% of its LNG capacity for three to five years<sup><a href="#ref-17">17</a></sup>. South Korea, which gets more than half its helium from Qatar<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>, kept its chip fabs running on months of inventory<sup><a href="#ref-46">46</a></sup>. A ceasefire reopened the Strait of Hormuz in June, but traffic is only partly back and attacks on tankers are rising again<sup><a href="#ref-47">47</a></sup>.
- **Sulfuric acid.** China halted exports of smelter by-product acid from May, citing fertiliser and food security; electronic-grade acid is exempt<sup><a href="#ref-42">42</a></sup>. Reports of the end date range from August to the end of the year<sup><a href="#ref-48">48</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-49">49</a></sup>. China exports only about 4% of the 110 Mt it makes<sup><a href="#ref-50">50</a></sup>, but Chile's leached copper depends on imported acid, and the benchmark price doubled in seven weeks<sup><a href="#ref-42">42</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-51">51</a></sup>.
- **Gallium and tungsten.** China makes about 99% of primary gallium. Outside China the price reached $1,850/kg in April while inside China it stayed flat<sup><a href="#ref-40">40</a></sup>. Tungsten rose about 350% in four months as China licensed just fifteen exporters<sup><a href="#ref-39">39</a></sup>.

None of these were about the crust running out.

It is also worth being clear about what *actually* limited the AI build-out this year: chips and memory. TSMC's most advanced lines have run near and even above 100% utilisation since late 2025, and SK Hynix's chief executive says the shortage of high-bandwidth memory (HBM, the stacked memory beside AI chips) could last until 2030<sup><a href="#ref-52">52</a></sup>. A new memory fab now takes three to five years<sup><a href="#ref-15">15</a></sup>. Gas turbines have a backlog of about 220 GW against factories that build 60–70 GW a year<sup><a href="#ref-53">53</a></sup>. One Japanese company, Nittobo, makes about 85% of the special glass cloth inside AI chip packages, and relief is not expected before 2027–28<sup><a href="#ref-16">16</a></sup>. Another, Ajinomoto, makes almost all of the ABF insulating film, and in August reportedly cut supply to mainland China by 30%<sup><a href="#ref-54">54</a></sup>. These are factories, not geology — but they are exactly the kind of slow, concentrated capacity this post is about.

## By-product traps

The hardest chokepoints to fix are materials nobody mines on purpose. Helium comes out of natural gas processing. Sulfuric acid comes out of oil refining and metal smelting. Gallium comes out of alumina refineries, germanium and indium out of zinc refineries, hafnium out of zirconium. Their supply is set by how much of the *host* product the world makes, not by their own price.

There is good news buried here: existing bauxite and zinc streams could yield several times more gallium than they do today, if someone builds the recovery circuits<sup><a href="#ref-38">38</a></sup>. And there is a twist that, as far as I can tell, nobody has written down. The growth models all have the economy electrify and leave fossil fuels behind<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>. That shrinks exactly the refineries and gas plants that produce sulfur and helium as by-products. The takeoff path itself tightens these traps. (This is my inference, not a finding from any of the sources.)

## Who holds the levers

The usual picture is China and everyone else. The real map has more players, and more of them pulled a lever in the last few years than most coverage suggests.

**China's controls are real, and Beijing frames them as normal.** Its commerce ministry calls them a "legitimate action… to refine its export control system", stresses that "export controls are not export bans", and points out that the US control list has over 3,000 items to China's roughly 900<sup><a href="#ref-57">57</a></sup>. Even a CFR report written to counter Chinese leverage concedes that China's controls have usually followed US tariffs or chip-export restrictions<sup><a href="#ref-58">58</a></sup>. Since March, a new State Council order gives Beijing legal grounds to retaliate against foreign firms that cut off Chinese suppliers<sup><a href="#ref-59">59</a></sup>. US imports of controlled rare earths have still not recovered to pre-control levels<sup><a href="#ref-60">60</a></sup>.

**China is also exposed.** It imports over 80% of the copper concentrate it smelts<sup><a href="#ref-50">50</a></sup>, over 45% of its sulfur, more than half of that through Hormuz<sup><a href="#ref-61">61</a></sup>, and in the first seven months of 2026 it imported more rare-earth material by weight than it exported<sup><a href="#ref-62">62</a></sup>. Its acid ban was as much self-protection as leverage. And its control leaks: provinces protect local revenue and smuggling rises with price gaps<sup><a href="#ref-63">63</a></sup>.

**Japan is the main counterweight.** Its firms make about 44% of silicon wafers, around three quarters of photoresist, most of the glass cloth and insulating film in AI chip packages, and most of the world's precision robot gears<sup><a href="#ref-64">64</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-65">65</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-16">16</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-54">54</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-26">26</a></sup>. China's dual-use export ban on Japan since January cut Japan's first-half dysprosium imports by 82%<sup><a href="#ref-66">66</a></sup>; Beijing's state media present it as a check on Japanese "remilitarization", not on Japanese people<sup><a href="#ref-67">67</a></sup>. Japan's tungsten users responded by importing scrap and building recycling — the 9DashLine argues the controls are backfiring<sup><a href="#ref-68">68</a></sup>. Meanwhile China Rare Earth Group reported net profit up 46.5% on lower revenue<sup><a href="#ref-62">62</a></sup>. Both can be true.

**Producer countries see value, not leverage.** UNCTAD counts 98 export measures on critical minerals since 2020 — bans, quotas, taxes and licences. The DRC has imposed the most (18), then China (16), then Indonesia (12)<sup><a href="#ref-69">69</a></sup>. The DRC caps cobalt exports at 87,000 tonnes a year plus a strategic reserve, to "preserve market balance"<sup><a href="#ref-55">55</a></sup>. Indonesia is pushing nickel processing at home — its president launched eleven downstream projects this month — while its own experts warn that high-grade ore could run out within a decade<sup><a href="#ref-70">70</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-71">71</a></sup>. The African Development Bank notes that Africa holds about 30% of critical-mineral deposits but captures a negligible share of the value<sup><a href="#ref-72">72</a></sup>. One honest caveat: I found no producer government that frames AI or robots as its opportunity. They talk about electric cars and the energy transition.

**Middle powers are hedging.** Korean defence firms are routing rare earths through Russia and Arab states<sup><a href="#ref-73">73</a></sup>. Chile's lower house passed a resolution warning that a US minerals deal must not restrict trade with China or create preferential access or regulated prices<sup><a href="#ref-74">74</a></sup>. India is pursuing "strategic autonomy" and a processing-first strategy; ORF notes reports that Chinese officials have asked foreign companies for product-design information in exchange for access to minerals<sup><a href="#ref-75">75</a></sup>. Bruegel urges Europe to be cautious about US-led price floors<sup><a href="#ref-76">76</a></sup>. And China has buyer power too: a single state purchaser now coordinates its iron-ore imports<sup><a href="#ref-77">77</a></sup>.

**Perception is itself a lever.** The 2010 China–Japan rare-earth "embargo" is not clearly visible in the trade data, yet it shaped fifteen years of Japanese policy, including the financing of Lynas<sup><a href="#ref-78">78</a></sup>. As of 28 September the US–China truce runs to 10 January 2027<sup><a href="#ref-79">79</a></sup>; analysts say a durable deal needs "actual deliveries of rare earths"<sup><a href="#ref-80">80</a></sup>, though the Chinese readout does not mention them<sup><a href="#ref-79">79</a></sup>.

## Why it matters: who goes first

If materials only slow the transition by a few years, why care? Because in a doubling economy, a few years is everything. Binder points out that a two-year head start in an economy that doubles yearly means four times the production<sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>. Forethought argues that a full-stack intelligence explosion may favour countries with deep industrial bases and permissive regulation, naming China and the Gulf states<sup><a href="#ref-81">81</a></sup>. Put that next to the lever map and the conclusion is uncomfortable: the chokepoints of the next five years may not decide *whether* the physical loop closes, but they may well decide *where*.

One honest limit: a purely software intelligence explosion, where better AI improves AI on a fixed stock of chips, sidesteps almost all of this<sup><a href="#ref-81">81</a></sup>. This post is about the moment the loop needs atoms.

## What to watch

- **10 January 2027** — the US–China truce deadline<sup><a href="#ref-79">79</a></sup>.
- **Late 2026** — whether China's sulfuric-acid export halt ends on schedule<sup><a href="#ref-48">48</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-49">49</a></sup>.
- **Late 2026** — first commercial magnets from MP Materials' US plant; whether ex-China oxide finds buyers<sup><a href="#ref-21">21</a></sup>.
- **February 2027 and March 2028** — Japan's large-scale seabed rare-earth mud trial and feasibility verdict<sup><a href="#ref-82">82</a></sup>.
- **2027–28** — Nittobo's new glass-cloth capacity<sup><a href="#ref-16">16</a></sup>; Ajinomoto's film expansion from 2028<sup><a href="#ref-54">54</a></sup>.
- **End of 2027** — Grasberg, one of the world's largest copper mines, back at full output<sup><a href="#ref-83">83</a></sup>.
- **Robot production doubling time.** If humanoid output takes longer than two years to double, magnets never bind before the mid-2040s. If it holds at one year past 2030, they bind in the mid-2030s.
- **New supply methods** — bioleaching of low-grade copper (Rio Tinto's Nuton started at Johnson Camp in September)<sup><a href="#ref-84">84</a></sup>, AI-led exploration, and magnet chemistries without rare earths.

## Notes on method

The interactive model is deliberately simple: demand grows exponentially from a 2026 baseline; supply grows at a constant rate; a fixed share of it is available to the sector in question. It ignores prices, substitution dynamics, stockpiles and recycling, all of which would soften the crossing. Treat the dates as illustrations of sensitivity, not forecasts. Lead-time splits are rough. Some figures come from paywalled reports read through summaries, notably the Payne Institute study, and the helium restart and some prices rely on secondary sources. The research for this post was carried out with the help of AI research agents; every number links to its source, and corrections are welcome.

> **In short.** If AI starts improving itself, the story eventually runs through chips, power plants, grids, and robots that build robots. Growth models say raw materials won't stop this, and on geology they are probably right. What they leave open is how fast particular mines, refineries and magnet plants, in particular countries, can grow while demand doubles every year or two. Under steady supply growth, extra supply buys little time: if robot production doubles every eighteen months, doubling magnet supply delays the crunch by under two years. And 2026's material shocks came from chokepoints, not from the crust running out. China holds the biggest levers, but not all of them.

## The loop

Better models earn money that buys computing power to train better models. At some point AI systems can run factories that build robots, power plants, chip fabs and more factories.

The loop runs at the speed of its slowest stage. Training a model takes months; a data centre, a year or two; a copper mine, on average about seventeen years from discovery to first production<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>. So my question is: *once the loop needs physical things, which inputs grow slowest, and who controls them?*

## What the growth models get right

Forethought's *Industrial Explosion* expects a robot economy to double initially in about a year (its appendix says "on the order of a few years") and guesses resources won't bind, though "that doesn't preclude resources being a bottleneck along the way"<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>. *AI 2027* has its robot economy doubling in under a year "so long as it didn't start to run out of raw materials"<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>. Damon Binder models annual production in every sector of the US economy, construction lags and the transition from today's economy<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>, and concludes: "Mining is only about 1% of output, so even large cost increases do little to slow overall growth"<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>. Epoch, modelling robot factories and components, finds that precision gear reducers bind first, at roughly half a million humanoids a year today, rising to 1.5–3 million by 2030<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup>.

I agree: known copper endowment grew about 25-fold between 1900 and 2010<sup><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup>, and energy-transition demand sits below known resources for almost every mineral; the problem is building mines and refineries fast enough<sup><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup>. What the models leave open:

1. **Ramp rates by material and place.** Dysprosium separation outside China is measured in tens of tonnes a year, and a new refinery must be built and approved by its customers before its output counts.
2. **Trade.** Binder's economy is closed<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>, Epoch's chip work stops above the minerals<sup><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup>, and none of the models represents an export ban, a refinery in only one country, or a missile strike on a gas plant.
3. **Getting there.** Binder's answer to dysprosium, "the most binding element", is to mine far more ore than needed<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>. That works once the industrial base exists; this post is about the years spent building it.

Minerals studies have the opposite blind spot: they take refining concentration seriously but assume smooth demand, with Wood Mackenzie's data-centre additions rising from 15–20 GW a year now to a peak of 30–33 GW in the early 2030s<sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-14">14</a></sup>.

## The clock

Much of what looks like a lead time is a queue or paperwork<sup><a href="#ref-15">15</a></sup>. A large transformer takes months to build; the two-year wait is the order book. Most of a copper mine's seventeen years go on exploration, studies and permits.

## Exponentials against mines

When does a demand that doubles on a fixed schedule outgrow the share it can claim of a supply that grows a few percent a year?

<figure>
  <MaterialRace />
  <figcaption>Exponential demand against slowly growing supply, on a log scale. Sources, and which defaults are my own assumptions, are listed under each tab. The doubling time is the hypothesis being tested, not a forecast.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Extra supply buys little time.** If demand doubles every $T_d$ years and supply grows at a fixed rate $g$, doubling the supply available pushes the crunch back by

$$
\Delta t = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln 2 / T_d - \ln(1+g)}
$$

years. For robots doubling every 18 months against magnet supply growing 8% a year, that is about 1.8 years; ten times more supply buys about six. Scarcity prices would speed supply up, but even at 16% a year, doubling supply buys only about 2.2 years.

**The date is set mostly by the doubling time.** Take 75,000–90,000 humanoids built in 2026<sup><a href="#ref-19">19</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-20">20</a></sup> (I round to 100,000), 2.5 kg of neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnet each, and about 226 kt of NdFeB made a year<sup><a href="#ref-21">21</a></sup>. With supply fixed, a one-year doubling takes robots to a fifth of today's magnet output around 2033 and all of it around 2036; a two-year doubling, around 2041 and 2046. At the chart's defaults the crunch lands around 2040.

A one-year doubling is not fanciful for the next decade. Goldman Sachs expects 6.5 million humanoids in 2035 and Bank of America 10 million<sup><a href="#ref-19">19</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-20">20</a></sup>: on average, a doubling every 1.3–1.4 years from 2026. CRU's base case of about 151 million robots a year in 2040 needs about 363 kt of magnets, around 160% of today's market<sup><a href="#ref-21">21</a></sup>. *AI 2027*'s milestone of a million new robots a month<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> would need 24–36 kt a year at humanoid-like magnet use: 11–16% of today's market.

### Where magnets bind

Not at the mine. Outside China, refined neodymium-praseodymium oxide is in surplus, at about $95/kg in August, below the $110/kg floor the US Defense Department guarantees MP Materials and Lynas, because too few magnet plants exist to buy it<sup><a href="#ref-22">22</a></sup>. The IEA calls magnet production "the main bottleneck for supply diversification"<sup><a href="#ref-23">23</a></sup>; new plants take years and specialised equipment, and, as Chinese commentators note, must compete on cost with China's mature industry<sup><a href="#ref-24">24</a></sup>.

Heavy rare earths, which let magnets survive heat, are tighter. Lynas, the only commercial separator of dysprosium and terbium outside China, produced 26 t in its December quarter and 8 t in its March quarter<sup><a href="#ref-25">25</a></sup>, against nameplate capacity of 240 t of dysprosium and 50 t of terbium a year, feedstock permitting<sup><a href="#ref-26">26</a></sup>. At the midpoint of the Foundation for American Innovation's 35–79 g of dysprosium-equivalent per humanoid, a million robots a year need about 57 t, though robots use far less per kilogram of magnet than electric cars<sup><a href="#ref-27">27</a></sup>.

### What binds before magnets

Gears and hands. One Japanese firm, Harmonic Drive Systems, holds about 80% of the strain-wave gear market<sup><a href="#ref-28">28</a></sup>, and Tesla's Optimus ramp has been slowed by fragile hands, supplier quality and weak software<sup><a href="#ref-29">29</a></sup>. Such problems move fast, but designs trade constraints: direct-drive joints swap gears for magnet, and Adamas expects magnet per robot to rise as robots gain joints<sup><a href="#ref-30">30</a></sup>.

### Copper

Inside a data centre, copper runs to about 12–47 tonnes per megawatt<sup><a href="#ref-31">31</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup>; one study puts about 64% of the copper tied to AI data centres in transmission and distribution, so counting the grid nearly triples it<sup><a href="#ref-14">14</a></sup>. S&P puts data-centre copper demand at about 1.1 Mt in 2025 and 2.5 Mt by 2040, against roughly 28 Mt of refined copper demand<sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>. At forecasters' growth rates that is a price story, not a wall: only 35–50% of announced capacity is reaching power on schedule<sup><a href="#ref-32">32</a></sup>, and 800-volt DC power and fibre interconnects cut copper use<sup><a href="#ref-33">33</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-34">34</a></sup>. Copper binds in the chart only if additions keep doubling, as AI improving itself would imply.

## Cost share and weak links

Materials are a tiny share of AI's costs: copper is under 0.5% to about 6% of data-centre capital spending, depending on scope<sup><a href="#ref-35">35</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-36">36</a></sup>, and helium for chipmaking is worth about $1 billion a year<sup><a href="#ref-37">37</a></sup>. If a 1% input gets ten times dearer, total costs rise about 10%.

But an essential input with no substitute hits harder than its cost share. Baqaee and Farhi show such non-linearities roughly tripled the macroeconomic impact of the 1970s oil shocks<sup><a href="#ref-38">38</a></sup>; Aghion, Jones and Jones argue growth is limited "not by what we do well but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve"<sup><a href="#ref-39">39</a></sup>. Near zero cost share, the threat is availability, not price<sup><a href="#ref-40">40</a></sup>. Gallium is a negligible share of GDP but an essential input for a particular device architecture (gallium-nitride power chips), and a restriction can interrupt qualified supply however little it costs.

So materials are a **speed limit**, not a wall: the slow, essential inputs set the pace, and where they are concentrated, they decide who goes first.

## 2026: a year of chokepoints

Each big material price shock of 2026 had a specific, non-geological cause (though shocks that make headlines are, by selection, the sudden ones).

<figure>
  <PriceShocks />
  <figcaption>Selected price comparisons, each with its own baseline (in the label), so bars are not strictly comparable. Tungsten<sup><a href="#ref-41">41</a></sup>; gallium<sup><a href="#ref-42">42</a></sup>; helium<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>; transformers<sup><a href="#ref-43">43</a></sup>; sulfuric acid in Chile<sup><a href="#ref-44">44</a></sup>; copper<sup><a href="#ref-45">45</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-46">46</a></sup>. A large gap between prices inside and outside China is the signature of export policy, not scarcity.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **War.** In March, Iranian strikes on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex took out about a third of world helium supply<sup><a href="#ref-47">47</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> and 17% of Qatar's LNG capacity for three to five years<sup><a href="#ref-18">18</a></sup>. South Korean chipmakers, who get over half their helium from Qatar<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>, said in late March they had months of inventory<sup><a href="#ref-48">48</a></sup>. Since a June ceasefire Hormuz has partly recovered: about 124 non-Iranian-linked transits a week in late September, against more than 100 a day before the war<sup><a href="#ref-79">79</a></sup>. Crude exports are near pre-crisis levels through costly workarounds, and tanker attacks are at a seven-month high<sup><a href="#ref-49">49</a></sup>.
- **Export controls.** China halted exports of smelter by-product sulfuric acid in May<sup><a href="#ref-44">44</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-50">50</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-51">51</a></sup>; it exports only about 4% of its output<sup><a href="#ref-52">52</a></sup>, but Chile's copper leaching depends on imported acid, and the price doubled in seven weeks<sup><a href="#ref-44">44</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-53">53</a></sup>. Gallium outside China reached $1,850/kg in April while staying flat inside<sup><a href="#ref-42">42</a></sup>, and tungsten rose about 350% in four months as China cut quotas and licensed fifteen exporters<sup><a href="#ref-41">41</a></sup>.
- **A single point of failure.** In August copper traded near record highs, well above analysts' forecasts, with Grasberg in Indonesia, one of the world's largest copper mines, still far below capacity after a 2025 mudflow<sup><a href="#ref-45">45</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-46">46</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-54">54</a></sup>.

What limited the AI build-out most this year was factory capacity, not geology. TSMC's most advanced lines have run at or above full utilisation since late 2025, SK Hynix's chief executive expects the high-bandwidth memory shortage to last until 2030<sup><a href="#ref-55">55</a></sup>, and a new memory fab takes three to five years<sup><a href="#ref-16">16</a></sup>. Gas turbines have a 220 GW backlog against 60–70 GW a year of output<sup><a href="#ref-56">56</a></sup>. Nittobo makes about 85% of the special glass cloth in AI chip packages, with no relief before 2027–28<sup><a href="#ref-17">17</a></sup>; Ajinomoto makes almost all of the ABF insulating film and reportedly cut supply to mainland China by 30% in August<sup><a href="#ref-57">57</a></sup>.

## By-product traps

The hardest chokepoints to fix are materials nobody mines on purpose, such as helium from natural gas, sulfur and acid from refining and smelting, or gallium from alumina refineries. Their supply depends mainly on how much of the *host* product the world makes, and on whether anyone invests in recovering them.

Blue: held back by policy (the DRC's cobalt quota responds to oversupply)<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-40">40</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-58">58</a></sup>.</figcaption>

Existing bauxite and zinc streams could yield several times more gallium with recovery circuits<sup><a href="#ref-40">40</a></sup>. But some hosts are shrinking: Maslin, Van Heerde and Day warn that phasing out fossil fuels removes the source of over 80% of the world's sulfur just as demand for sulfuric acid rises<sup><a href="#ref-59">59</a></sup>. A fast, AI-run electrification of the economy<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> would deepen that squeeze, and possibly a similar one for helium.

## Who holds the levers

China holds the biggest: about 91% of rare-earth refining and 94% of sintered magnet output<sup><a href="#ref-23">23</a></sup>, and about 99% of primary gallium<sup><a href="#ref-42">42</a></sup>. But Japan, Taiwan, Korea and the US hold many downstream levers, from wafers and photoresist to memory, chip-package materials and robot gears, and producer countries have their own.

**China's controls are real, and Beijing frames them as normal.** Its commerce ministry insists "export controls are not export bans"<sup><a href="#ref-61">61</a></sup>, and even a CFR report written to counter Chinese leverage concedes that they have usually followed US tariffs or chip-export restrictions<sup><a href="#ref-62">62</a></sup>. Since March, a State Council order lets Beijing retaliate against foreign firms that cut off Chinese suppliers<sup><a href="#ref-63">63</a></sup>.

**China is also exposed.** It imports over 80% of the copper concentrate it smelts<sup><a href="#ref-52">52</a></sup> and over 45% of its sulfur, more than half through Hormuz<sup><a href="#ref-64">64</a></sup>, so its acid ban was as much self-protection as leverage. Its control also leaks: provinces protect local revenue, and smuggling follows price gaps<sup><a href="#ref-65">65</a></sup>.

**Japan is the main counterweight.** Its firms make about 44% of silicon wafers, around three quarters of photoresist, most chip-package glass cloth and insulating film, and most precision robot gears<sup><a href="#ref-66">66</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-67">67</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-17">17</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-57">57</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-28">28</a></sup>. China's January ban on dual-use exports to Japanese military end users coincided with an 82% fall in Japan's first-half imports of dysprosium raw mineral<sup><a href="#ref-24">24</a></sup>; Japanese tungsten users, meanwhile, turned to scrap and recycling, and 9DashLine argues the controls are backfiring<sup><a href="#ref-68">68</a></sup>.

**Producers and middle powers have levers too.** UNCTAD counts 98 export restrictions on critical minerals since 2020, most by the DRC, China and Indonesia<sup><a href="#ref-69">69</a></sup>. The DRC caps cobalt exports to "preserve market balance" amid oversupply<sup><a href="#ref-58">58</a></sup>; Indonesia keeps nickel processing at home<sup><a href="#ref-70">70</a></sup>. Korean defence firms route rare earths through Russia and Arab states<sup><a href="#ref-71">71</a></sup>, and Chile's lower house warned that a US minerals deal must not restrict trade with China<sup><a href="#ref-72">72</a></sup>. No producer government I found frames AI or robots as its opportunity.

Perception is itself a lever: the 2010 China–Japan rare-earth "embargo" barely shows in the trade data, yet it shaped fifteen years of Japanese policy, including the financing of Lynas<sup><a href="#ref-73">73</a></sup>.

## Why it matters: who goes first

In an economy that doubles yearly, a two-year head start means four times the production<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>. Forethought argues that an AI boom that also automates physical industry may favour countries with deep industrial bases and permissive regulation, naming China and the Gulf states<sup><a href="#ref-74">74</a></sup>. So the chokepoints of the next five years may not decide *whether* the physical loop closes, but may well decide *where*.

One limit: if AI improves itself purely in software, on a fixed stock of chips, almost none of this applies<sup><a href="#ref-74">74</a></sup>.

## What to watch

- **Robot production doubling time.** If humanoid output takes two years or more to double, robots won't need even a fifth of today's magnet output before the 2040s; if it doubles yearly past 2030, they'll need all of it by the mid-2030s.
- **Late 2026 to January 2027**: the end of China's acid export halt<sup><a href="#ref-50">50</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-51">51</a></sup>, buyers for ex-China rare-earth oxide<sup><a href="#ref-22">22</a></sup>, and the US–China truce deadline on 10 January, where analysts say a durable deal needs "actual deliveries of rare earths"<sup><a href="#ref-75">75</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-76">76</a></sup>.
- **2027–28**: Nittobo's new glass cloth<sup><a href="#ref-17">17</a></sup>; Ajinomoto's film expansion from 2028, with a third plant around 2032<sup><a href="#ref-57">57</a></sup>; Japan's seabed rare-earth mud trial<sup><a href="#ref-77">77</a></sup>; Grasberg's return to full output, targeted for end-2027 but possibly early 2028<sup><a href="#ref-54">54</a></sup>.
- **New supply**: bioleaching of low-grade copper<sup><a href="#ref-78">78</a></sup>, AI-led exploration, rare-earth-free magnets and motors.

## Notes on method

The interactive model is deliberately simple: exponential demand, supply growing at a constant rate, and a fixed share available to the sector. It ignores prices, substitution, stockpiles and recycling, all of which would soften the crossing, so the dates show sensitivity, not forecasts. My own assumptions are labelled under each tab.

> **In short.** Several middle powers are writing laws on frontier AI and data centres right now: Singapore, Japan, South Korea, Australia and the European Union. If each of them wrote the same six safety conditions into its own law, and recognised the others' checks, a growing part of the world's frontier AI work would run under rules that are tested independently, reported jointly, and cannot be switched off by a phone call from abroad. That is a stronger guarantee than any statement, because it binds through courts, licences and buildings rather than signatures. The decisions are scheduled in the next eight months.

This post is written for people who can act on one of those decisions: officials drafting the laws, the institutes that would do the testing, the governments that launched last month's *Call for Control of Frontier AI Models*, and the funders and researchers who support them. It follows from [an earlier post on the raw materials behind the AI build-out](/en/blog/speed-limits-not-walls), which looked at who holds physical leverage. This one asks how to turn leverage into something that lasts.

## Why statements are not enough

On 21 September, the leaders of Finland and Norway launched *A Call for Control of Frontier AI Models*. It asks companies for mandatory pre-deployment testing by independent evaluators, asks governments to share reports of serious incidents, and asks the UN to explore an institution "able to set standards, enable verification, and convene states when capability thresholds are crossed". Thirty countries and the European Commission have signed it<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>.

The same week, the United States gathered signatures for a *Joint Statement on AI Opportunity Partnership*. It commits signatories to "pro-innovation regulation" and to aligning their AI supply chains with those of the United States. Fifty-four countries have signed that one<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>. At the UN General Assembly, the US President said the United States rejects any global framework for controlling AI<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>.

**Nineteen countries signed both**, including Germany, the Netherlands, Australia and Singapore. The two documents point in opposite directions. That a country can sign both tells us what a signature is worth: very little, because it costs nothing to give and nothing to ignore.

Agreements fare little better under pressure. In January, the United States threatened rising tariffs on European allies until it could acquire Greenland<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>. Europe debated using its Anti-Coercion Instrument and did not<sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>. In September, Denmark and Greenland signed an amendment to their 1951 defence agreement that expands US military rights on the island with no end date<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>. In June, the US government ordered Anthropic to cut off non-US users from its most capable models<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup>; the EU's cyber-security agency got access three months after release<sup><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup>. A bill in Congress would make US penalties apply to allies whose export rules on chip-making equipment differ from Washington's<sup><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup>. And holding a real chokepoint does not help on its own: when Japan restricted exports of three chip-making chemicals to Korea in 2019, Korea built its own supply, Japanese suppliers lost market share, and the policy goal was not met<sup><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup>.

What does survive pressure? Four things:

| | A signed statement | A condition written into law |
|---|---|---|
| **Automatic** — works without a new decision when pressure arrives | No: someone must choose to act on it | Yes: the licence condition applies until changed |
| **Hands tied** — giving in is legally hard | No | Yes: changing it takes legislation or a court |
| **Immovable** — rests on something that cannot be moved or switched off from abroad | No | Yes: the data centre is on your soil, under your licence |
| **Pooled** — no single country can be singled out | Only on paper | Yes, if several countries write the *same* conditions and recognise each other's checks |

A statement has none of these properties. A shared condition in several countries' laws has all four.

## The six conditions

Each condition applies to companies that train frontier models, or host them, above a size threshold in the country passing the law. None of them requires a treaty.

1. **Independent testing access.** Evaluators from a recognised AI safety institute in *any* participating country can test a frontier model trained or hosted on that soil before it is released. The EU already has this power for its own evaluations: since August it can demand access to a model's weights and hosting infrastructure<sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup>.
2. **Shared incident reporting.** Serious safety incidents are reported within a fixed deadline to the host country *and* to every other participating country. Korea's AI Basic Act, Singapore's new data-centre law and Australia's proposed standards already contain incident-reporting duties<sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-15">15</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-16">16</a></sup>; the change is to share the reports.
3. **Protection of model weights.** Weights trained or stored on the country's soil meet a common security standard, so that a frontier model cannot be stolen from a member's data centre more easily than from its developer's own.
4. **Guaranteed service for essential users.** Government and critical-infrastructure users in the host country keep access to models running there, including during a crisis. June showed that access granted by a foreign government can be withdrawn overnight<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup>.
5. **Known chip locations.** Operators can show where their advanced chips are, and export licences for chips and their key inputs carry the same condition. This is what makes any future agreement between the great powers verifiable.
6. **A local off-switch.** The operator can isolate or shut down a frontier workload on lawful order of the host country.

None of this is exotic. The United States already attaches security, customer-checking and no-diversion conditions to the advanced chips it lets the United Arab Emirates host, and those conditions expire unless Washington renews them<sup><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup>. Conditions 3, 5 and 6 copy that format. A government that asks the same of data centres on its own soil is doing what Washington already does, which makes the conditions hard to call illegitimate.

## What changes if all six pass

Suppose Singapore, Japan, Korea, Australia and the EU write these conditions into the instruments they are drafting this winter, and agree that a test by one country's institute counts in all of them. By the time of the Geneva AI Summit in June 2027, the result would be:

- **No frontier model trained or served in those countries escapes independent testing.** Testing no longer depends on access a foreign government can revoke. Because one test counts everywhere, developers face one process rather than five, which makes compliance cheaper than avoidance.
- **An early-warning network for incidents.** A serious failure seen in Seoul is known in Canberra, Tokyo, Singapore and Brussels within days, not after a press report.
- **Fewer places to steal a frontier model from.** Weights on member soil are protected to a common standard.
- **Governments keep the tools they depend on.** Cyber defence and public services in member countries cannot be cut off by a decision taken elsewhere.
- **The groundwork for verification.** Known chip locations and local off-switches are exactly what an international body needs in order to check that commitments are kept. The *Call for Control* asks for such an institution; these conditions would give it something to verify.
- **A coalition that holds under pressure.** Each government's commitment sits in its own law, enforced by its own courts and regulators, on data centres it physically hosts. Breaking it means changing a law in public, not quietly not acting on a statement. And because the conditions are shared, pressure on one member is pressure on a common standard.

This would not govern compute inside the United States or China. But developers who want to train, host or sell in these markets would meet the same tested, reported and verifiable standard, and that standard would be in place before the next generation of models, not after.

Two things tie these together. First, **the same text**: if the conditions are drafted once, each country can adapt the wording to its own legal system without changing the substance. Second, **recognition of each other's checks**: an evaluation by Korea's institute counts in Singapore, a report filed in Australia reaches Japan. Add a simple rule that no member licenses a frontier cluster another has refused without consulting first, and the coalition cannot be picked apart one country at a time.

## What I'm asking

- **If you are drafting one of these instruments**, include the six conditions, or the subset that fits, and write in recognition of tests by other participating countries' institutes.
- **If you work at an AI safety institute**, agree with your counterparts that an evaluation by one counts for all, and say so publicly.
- **If your government launched or signed the *Call for Control***, turn it into a working group on these conditions, with Geneva as the deadline. Signatures have been collected; the next step is shared text.
- **If you work near a government that hasn't joined** — Japan especially, which is reviewing its AI law now and holds more of the supply chain than any other middle power — make the case that writing these conditions into law serves Japan's own security, whatever it signs.
- **If you fund this work**, the most useful thing to pay for now is the drafting: one set of model conditions with legal versions for each jurisdiction, ready before the December deadlines.

## What could go wrong

The United States could forbid its companies from accepting foreign testing, as it restricted access in June. Then companies might simply not train in these countries, so the coalition must be worth more as a host and a market than staying away. Trade agreements that ban data-localisation rules mean the conditions must be drafted as security measures. The host country's leverage is strongest while frontier computing is scarce and fades if it becomes plentiful. And the hardest part is the pooling: each country will be tempted to make its own deal. These risks are real, but each is smaller than the risk of relying on signatures.

*Updated 27 October 2026. The warning-aligned records were rechecked on that date, through the selected restoration on 26 October. Earlier supporting sources retain their stated dates; these notices are not live service-status reports.*

> **In short.** The Japanese cases do not describe one uniform shutdown response. Pixiv isolated services early and reopened the same night; Asken started near the scheduled release and returned the next morning. Sakura separated functions and responsibilities. The Korean FFXIV service adds a clear example: login returned more than three days before settings backup. **Recovery is a function-specific endpoint**, not a single company-wide clock. Compare when isolation began, what it isolated, and what remained unresolved.

## One warning, several recovery clocks

Aligning these responses to PHP's **public maintainer advance warning**, at 19:33:47 UTC on 20 October, reveals another distinction. The warning is a documented reference—not the first-ever disclosure, full technical disclosure or patch availability. The scheduled release follows about 18h 26m later.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>

**The same incident can have more than one recovery time.** Korean FFXIV's new-login restriction lasted 25h 25m, but settings backup remained restricted for another **3d 15h 18m**, until 26 October 18:08 (assumed KST). Those are two functions of one response, not independent outages or whole-game downtime; the notices attribute the work to PHP without naming exact CVEs. Two earlier game/launcher maintenance pairs provide context: 4h planned versus 3h 25m reported completed, and 5h versus 4h 25m. Their different scope rules out a like-for-like downtime ratio.<sup><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-14">14</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-15">15</a></sup>

## Japan faced an overnight decision—not a single response

PHP announced security releases for 21 October at 14:00 UTC: **23:00 in Japan**.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> That was a scheduled public release, not a timestamp for every distributor's package or each operator's receipt. It nevertheless provides a common reference for reconstructing the Japanese decisions.

Pixiv reports taking pixiv, Encyclopedia and FANBOX offline at 19:00 JST, applying updates after disclosure, completing checks and reopening at 23:30. Asken reports stopping its apps, website and corporate services around 22:50 and restoring all of them at 09:25 the next morning after patching and safety checks.<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

**Starting isolation earlier and completing recovery later are different decisions.** In this pair, the earlier closure also reopened earlier. The records do not reveal the patching/testing split, staffing, configuration difficulty or whether either operator could safely have stayed online. Calling the difference “Japanese overreaction” would erase variation *within Japan* before explaining it. Conversely, the overnight schedule does not prove that night staffing caused delay.

## The overlooked difference is the containment boundary

There is a second distinction in the same notices. Asken's declared scope covers all functions of its apps, website and corporate services. Pixiv's notice extends beyond three named products: some functions in other services using pixiv accounts were also unavailable.<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Counting company names—or even product names—misses the affected dependency boundary.

Sakura provides a different pattern. Its corporate update says necessary responses were complete, including fixes to the PHP versions it supplied, while some cloud functions remained suspended. The linked appliance notice identifies the disabled web administration interfaces and offers compatible SQL clients as the alternative; Database Cluster is excluded.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>

Sakura also says its VPS service-provisioning layer did not use PHP and was unaffected, **while customers running PHP inside their VPS still needed to update it**.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> “Provider unaffected,” “provider patched” and “customer protected” are therefore three different claims. Likewise, the SQL-client alternative is evidence of a narrower response option—not proof that every customer switched successfully or retained every workflow. The notice gives no restart date for the web UI; that is not permanent retirement.

This changes the practical question. Instead of asking whether Japanese operators should shut down less, ask **which dependency can be isolated without disabling the essential function, and who owns the remaining fix**. The notices establish that these boundaries differed. They do not establish their relative security or cost.

## Benchmark the same outcome across countries

The first useful Japan–Korea pair is **managed hosting: Sakura and Cafe24**, not their corporate websites. Cafe24's work notice names the same two CVEs and plans staged official patching with **1–10 minutes of downtime per server**. Its 23 October follow-up reports patch application completed for PHP 7.4 and newer, but does not measure the interruption actually experienced by each customer.<sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup>

Sources: service-specific sections of the two operators' notices.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup> **The established similarity is the responsibility boundary; the disclosed rollout detail differs.** Neither “Sakura patched” nor “Cafe24 patched” covers every customer's software. The missing benchmark is actual interruption and functional validation for comparable managed-hosting configurations—not another count of company shutdown notices.

BambooHR makes the interval between patching and validation visible. It reports an applied fix at 15:03 UTC and restored access at 18:01: a 178-minute **reporting gap**, not measured testing time. Its 11:51–18:01 report span is not an outage duration either; an undated edit prevents a reliable onset. A UK minister also reported 32 government services unavailable and four partly unavailable despite advance warning.<sup><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup> These records challenge a uniquely Japanese shutdown story, but cannot rank national response speed. They are not a census of affected PHP installations. Unknown outcomes are not “no downtime.”

There is also a reason to follow notices forward. ValueDesign's 22 October PHP notice says Value Card ASP was operating while two other services were suspended. Its separate 23 October notice discloses unauthorised access and data leakage involving Value Card; the parent-company disclosure dates confirmation to 19 October and attributes it only to a web-server vulnerability.<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> The exact PHP linkage and deployment mapping are unestablished. This is **not** a confirmed PHP-exploitation case for our comparison, nor evidence that “operating” means “uncompromised.”

## Faster warning and cheaper code are hypotheses to test

Our bounded Hacker News check found an important trade-off. A sampled PHP heads-up admits unverified sourcing and gives a different release date; a later comment corrects it from php.net. Another item was created on 20 October but now links the 21 October “update now” advisory. **Creation time is not proof that today's wording was available then.**<sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup> An English-language monitoring advantage therefore needs verified content histories and evidence of useful action, not just earlier timestamps. This two-incident check is not a predictive benchmark.

The same distinction applies to agents. PatchBench reports a large gap between stopping a supplied crash and passing both security and behaviour checks; it does not measure deployed protection.<sup><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup> That is supporting evidence for testing repairs, not an original result of this investigation or a substitute for the Japanese operator analysis.

**Japan's shutdown headlines conceal different isolation timings, dependency boundaries and unfinished responsibilities. Following the same warning across services adds a fourth distinction: which function counts as “recovered.” A useful benchmark must keep all four visible before interpreting speed.**
